Beginning: Establish Connection and Set the Agenda

  • Greet the patient by name.
  • Introduce yourself if this is your first meeting.
  • Make eye contact before interacting with the computer.
  • Confirm the patient and chart match before discussing medical information.
  • Sit down whenever practical.
  • Open with a human question before launching into the medical interview when appropriate; ideally personalized to the patient.
    • How has your day been going?
    • How's work?
    • How's retirement treating you?
    • How's the family doing?
    • How was that trip to the everglades?
    • Any plans for the holidays?
    • Are you still working on that hobby?
    • Did you get a chance to attend your daughter's graduation?
  • Ask what the patient wants to accomplish today.
  • Elicit the entire agenda before starting the first problem.
    • “Anything else you were hoping we could address today?”
  • Add your agenda: preventive care, vaccinations, follow-up needs, medication reconciliation, chart cleanup, etc.
  • Negotiate priorities explicitly if everything cannot reasonably be addressed.
    • Avoid discovering the patient's most important concern as your hand reaches for the door.

During: Demonstrate Attention and Partnership

  • Never communicate contempt. Not for smoking, obesity, substance use, nonadherence, low health literacy, bizarre internet theories, vaccine refusal, or a medication list they don't remember.
  • Let them finish their opening monologue uninterrupted if possible
  • Never appear rushed, even when rushed.
  • Don't check your watch
  • Don't rest your hand on the door
  • Avoid pressured speech
  • When saying "no", give them a "yes". "I don't think an MRI will be helpful. Instead, I think we should..."
  • Avoid medical jargon
  • Use the computer as a shared tool rather than a barrier between you and the patient.
  • Position the screen where both you and the patient can see it when practical.
  • Include the patient in what you are doing on the computer.
    • Review labs, imaging, medication lists, and prior notes together.
    • Let the patient see you place orders, referrals, and prescriptions.
    • Explain what you are looking for when reviewing the chart.
  • Shift your attention deliberately between the patient and the screen. Make eye contact during important parts of the conversation.
  • Avoid interrupting the patient's opening explanation unnecessarily.
  • Own mistakes immediately
  • Use brief verbal or nonverbal acknowledgments so the patient knows you are following them. Use empathetic statements, "That sounds awful."
  • Explain what you are doing on the computer when computer work becomes prolonged.
    • “I'm looking through your previous labs for a moment.”
  • Signpost transitions.
    • “We've covered the toenail. Let's move to your allergies.”
  • Explain your reasoning, particularly when recommending against something the patient expected.
  • Invite the patient into decisions when multiple reasonable options exist.
  • Acknowledge important emotions or concerns explicitly rather than responding only to the biomedical problem.
  • Perform appropriate physical contact/examination when clinically indicated.
    • A handshake, pulse examination, heart/lung examination, or other natural contact can make the encounter feel substantially less impersonal.
  • Preserve the patient's dignity during examination: explain what you are about to do, obtain permission where appropriate, and minimize unnecessary exposure.

End: Close the Loop

  • Signal that you are transitioning to the plan.
    • “Okay, let's put all of this together.”
  • Summarize what you think is happening in plain language.
  • Review what the patient is supposed to do next.
    • Medications started/stopped/changed
    • Tests or referrals
    • Self-care
    • Follow-up interval
    • Reasons to return sooner
  • Use teach-back for complicated or consequential plans.
    • “Just so I know I explained it clearly, tell me what you're going to do when you get home.”
  • Ask whether the plan is feasible.
    • Cost, transportation, schedule, pharmacy access, caregiving, and other barriers can turn an excellent plan into no plan at all.
  • Revisit the opening agenda.
    • “We talked about the toenail and renewed the allergy medication. Was there anything we said we'd cover that we missed?”
  • Ask for final questions.
  • State when/how follow-up will occur, rather than leaving it implicit.
  • Review the After Visit Summary rather than treating it as paperwork.
  • Ensure the patient receives the AVS electronically or in print according to their preference/workflow.
  • End with a clear warm closing statement so the patient knows the encounter is complete and what happens next. "Good seeing you. We'll get this figured out. I'm glad you came in."

Look the Part

  • Wear clean, professional attire appropriate to the clinic.
  • Consider wearing a white coat; many patients associate it with professionalism, competence, and trustworthiness.
  • Wear your name badge prominently and make it easy to identify you as the physician.
  • Keep the white coat and clothing clean and well maintained.
  • Use hand sanitizer upon entering the patient room
